\subsection{Metrics on Retrieval-conditioned Trajectory Efficiency}
Traditional evaluation protocols for RAG systems primarily focus on generation quality (e.g., EM, Accuracy, F1) and retrieval effectiveness (e.g., Recall, MRR, NDCG). However, these metrics overlook the \textbf{trajectory-level dynamics} of agentic pipelines, where multi-iteration query generation and retrieval decisions critically determine final outcomes. In particular, the quality of intermediate trajectories—i.e., whether each iteration with retrieval action contributes meaningful and relevant information—remains underexplored, despite being a key source of failure in agentic RAG systems.

We deliberately design Retrieval-Driven Trajectory Efficiency (RTE) as a retrieval-conditioned metric to isolate the effect of retrieval dynamics on trajectory evolution. We extract the action subsequences involving retrieval from the Pipeline action state sequence. Our metric decomposes trajectory efficiency into two complementary components: 
\textbf{(1) Information Increment}, which quantifies the additional information gained at each iteration, and 
\textbf{(2) Iteration Utility}, which evaluates the contribution of each retrieval step toward the final answer.

\paragraph{Information Increment.}
We quantify the information increment introduced at each iteration by measuring how many \textbf{new atomic facts} are brought by the current retrieval result, relative to the previously accumulated fact history. Let $\mathcal{F}_t$ denote the set of atomic facts extracted from the retrieval result at step $t$, and let $\mathcal{N}_t \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t$ denote the subset of facts that are semantically novel with respect to the historical fact set $\mathcal{F}_{<t}$. Here novelty is determined after exact and semantic deduplication against the trajectory history. We define the information increment at step $t$ as
\begin{equation}
    \Delta F_t = \dfrac{\left| \mathcal{N}_t \right|}{\left| \mathcal{F}_t \right| + \epsilon},
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ is a small constant for numerical stability. By construction, $\Delta F_t \in [0,1]$. If $\Delta F_t = 0$, the current iteration retrieves no semantically novel information. Furthermore, when consecutive text queries are nearly identical, the current iteration is unlikely to yield new information. In such cases, the retrieval process tends to return highly overlapping results, leading to redundant iterations without meaningful progress. Therefore, when the similarity exceeds a predefined threshold, the information increment is approximated as zero:
\begin{equation}
\Delta F_t
=
\mathbf{1}\!\left[\operatorname{sim}(q_t, q_{t-1}) \le \tau\right]
\cdot
\dfrac{\left| \mathcal{N}_t \right|}{\left| \mathcal{F}_t \right| + \epsilon}.
\end{equation}

While this metric captures whether the retrieval process makes progress in terms of factual novelty, it does not account for the \textbf{utility} of the newly acquired information with respect to resolving the query. Therefore, we introduce an additional metric to quantify how much of the novel information is actually useful for answering the current sub-question.
\paragraph{Iteration Utility.} To further assess whether an iteration contributes to the final answer, we introduce a metric termed \textit{Iteration Utility}. This metric leverages the intrinsic structure of the ReAct pipeline: 
the \texttt{<Thought>} at iteration $t_j$ ($j \geq 1$) is conditioned on the retrieval results obtained at step $t_{j-1}$. Therefore, the content of the \texttt{<Thought>} provides a direct signal of how the retrieved evidence is interpreted and utilized by the model. Compared to relying on external annotators or expert judgments, this approach evaluates utility from the model’s own reasoning perspective, ensuring consistency with the underlying execution dynamics of the ReAct pipeline. 
Consequently, it offers a more faithful characterization of how intermediate retrieval results influence the final answer generation.

Instead of assigning a coarse discrete label, we estimate utility at the fact level. Specifically, from the novel fact set $\mathcal{N}_t$, we ask an LLM to select the subset $\mathcal{H}_t \subseteq \mathcal{N}_t$ that is helpful for answering the current sub-question under the current post-retrieval \texttt{<Thought>}. Importantly, the LLM is instructed to select a \emph{maximal supporting subset}: a fact that appears weak in isolation may still be retained if it jointly supports the reasoning chain together with other facts. We then define iteration utility as the fraction of novel facts that are judged to be helpful:
\begin{equation}
    U_t = \dfrac{\left| \mathcal{H}_t \right|}{\left| \mathcal{N}_t \right| + \epsilon}.
\end{equation}
Thus, $U_t \in [0,1]$ measures the effective usefulness of the newly retrieved information rather than a coarse categorical judgment. The step-level trajectory score is then defined as the product of utility and information increment:
\begin{equation}
    S_t = U_t \cdot \Delta F_t
\end{equation}
To penalize "round idling", i.e., the situation where the Search Query remains the same in subsequent rounds, the overall Trajectory Quality Score (abbreviated as RTE) is computed as the average step-level score over the entire trajectory:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RTE} = \frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{t=1}^{|T|} S_t
\end{equation}
% A higher RTE indicates that the trajectory is more informative, more useful, and overall better aligned with successful task completion. If the subsequent rounds are repeated and no information or ultility is gained, the RTE of those scores are zero while the dominitator still increases, hence the overall RTE is still low.
A higher RTE indicates that the retrieval-conditioned sub-chain within the full trajectory is more informative, more useful, and better aligned with successful task completion. When later rounds repeatedly issue similar queries, retrieve little novel information, or produce novel facts that are mostly unhelpful for solving the sub-question, their step-level contributions remain low while the trajectory length continues to increase. Consequently, excessive repetitive or low-yield iterations lead to a lower overall RTE.
